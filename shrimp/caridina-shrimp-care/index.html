Caridina Shrimp Care: Crystal Red, Bee & Taiwan Bee Shrimp

By Pet Zone SD TeamUpdated November 28, 202611 min read

Caridina is the other main dwarf shrimp genus in the hobby, and it's a step up in difficulty from Neocaridina (cherry shrimp): narrower water parameters, a near-zero KH that needs an active substrate to hold steady, and — for the high-grade color lines like Crystal Red, Crystal Black, and Taiwan Bee — a grading system that affects price and breeding strategy as much as everyday care. None of that makes Caridina unreasonable to keep. It means the margin for sloppy water is much smaller than it is with cherry shrimp, so the setup has to be right before the shrimp go in.

Caridina vs. Neocaridina: what actually changes

Our Neocaridina shrimp care guide covers why cherry shrimp tolerate a wide band of everyday water conditions. Caridina cantonensis — the species behind Crystal Red, Crystal Black, Bee, and most Taiwan Bee lines — comes from different wild water and doesn't share that tolerance. The practical differences are water chemistry (softer, more acidic, far less buffered), substrate (an active, buffering substrate is close to mandatory rather than optional), and stability (smaller, less frequent water changes rather than Neocaridina's small-and-frequent approach). Molting, feeding on biofilm, and general colony behavior work the same way in both genera; it's the water itself that's the hard part with Caridina.

Water parameters

Sources that specialize in Caridina broadly agree on the direction — softer, more acidic, more tightly buffered water than Neocaridina — but not always on the exact numbers, especially at the upper end:

ParameterCommonly cited range
Temperature68–77°F
pH5.5–7.0
GH (general hardness)4–6 dGH
KH (carbonate hardness)0–2 dKH, kept as close to 0 as practical
TDS (total dissolved solids)100–180 ppm

Those figures come from Shrimp Science's Crystal Red Shrimp profile, which specifically warns that if KH runs much above that range shrimp "may struggle to molt safely." A second widely read source, 2Hr Aquarist's guide to keeping dwarf shrimp in planted tanks, gives a noticeably wider envelope for the same species group — pH up to 7.5, KH up to 4, and TDS as high as 300 ppm. We're not picking a winner here: treat the tighter Shrimp Science numbers as the safer target to aim for, and the wider 2Hr range as evidence that some strains and individual colonies do tolerate more drift than the strictest guidance suggests. What nobody disputes is the direction of travel from Neocaridina — lower KH, lower TDS, more acidic — and that getting KH close to zero is the one number worth taking seriously, since it's what makes pH hold steady at all. See our water parameters and testing guide for how to actually measure GH, KH, and TDS at home.

Because Caridina want water this soft, most keepers start from RO or RO/DI water and build it back up with a remineralizer formulated for shrimp rather than trying to soften San Diego tap water down to range. See our San Diego tap water guide for why local tap (hard and alkaline) is the wrong starting point for this genus without significant treatment.

Active substrate isn't optional here

A tank with KH near zero has almost no buffering capacity of its own — the water's chemistry is defenseless against anything that pushes on it, from a water change to a CO2 injection schedule. That's why Shrimp Science specifically recommends a "buffering or active substrate" for Crystal Red Shrimp rather than leaving pH management to water changes alone. An aquasoil substrate does double duty: it leaches a mild, steady acidity that holds pH down in the target range, and because it has little carbonate content of its own, it doesn't fight the near-zero KH the way crushed coral or a calcium-rich gravel would. Our aquarium substrate guide and beginner planted tank guide both cover aquasoil in more depth, including the ammonia bump it can cause in the first week or two — worth planning around before shrimp go in, not after.

CO2 and planted Caridina tanks

Caridina tanks are frequently also planted tanks, and CO2 injection interacts with this genus's water chemistry more directly than it does with hardier fish. 2Hr Aquarist notes that CO2 injection can pull pH down by as much as a full point on its own, but has little effect without some carbonate hardness already present to react against — which is part of why a near-zero-KH Caridina tank run on an aquasoil substrate behaves differently under CO2 than a typical community tank does. The same source also notes that delicate, specific-requirement shrimp lines can breed less readily in a CO2-injected tank than in a non-injected one, which is worth knowing before assuming more plant growth is purely a win for a breeding colony. If you're planning CO2 for the plants rather than the shrimp, our plant fertilizing guide and the CO2 injection glossary entry cover the basics; for Caridina specifically, a stable low-tech setup is the lower-risk starting point.

Water changes: smaller swings matter more than schedule

Our Neocaridina guide recommends small, frequent water changes — roughly 10–15% weekly — to avoid shocking a colony that tolerates a wide parameter band but not a sudden shift within it. Caridina guidance leans further in the same direction for a different reason: 2Hr Aquarist suggests that more delicate, specific-requirement lines may do better with changes as infrequent as 30% every two weeks rather than small weekly ones, provided the tank stays otherwise stable between changes. The two pieces of advice aren't actually in conflict — both are protecting against the same thing, a sudden parameter swing — they just reflect that a Caridina tank's water, once dialed in on RO/remineralizer and an active substrate, drifts more slowly than a tap-water Neocaridina tank does, so it needs correcting less often rather than more gently each time. Match tank water to replacement water in temperature, pH, and TDS before any change, regardless of how often you do it.

Grading: what C through SSS actually means

Crystal Red and Crystal Black Shrimp are graded on how much white versus red (or black) coverage they show and how clean the pattern is, not on health or hardiness. Based on The Shrimp Farm's grading guide, the scale runs roughly:

  • C grade: Nearly solid red (or black) body with only thin white stripes.
  • B grade: Blotchy coloring, with bands just starting to form.
  • A grade: Opaque, defined color bands.
  • S grade: More white coverage, with a less sharply defined middle band.
  • SS grade (Hinomaru): A solid red or black dot on the back, named for its resemblance to the red circle on Japan's flag.
  • SSS grade (Mosura): Nearly all-white body with a small red or black marking confined to the head, in named patterns like "flower," "crown," "heart," or "smiley."

There's a real quirk in how that scale is described: the general rule given is "more white is better, except for full white," yet the Mosura pattern — which is almost entirely white — sits at the very top of the scale (SSS). The resolution isn't a contradiction so much as a shift in what's being judged: once a shrimp is mostly white, grading stops rewarding white coverage itself and starts rewarding how sharp, symmetrical, and deliberately placed the remaining color is. A shrimp that's simply gone pale or lost pigment with age doesn't qualify the same way a clean Mosura pattern does, even though both are mostly white. S+ variants like "tiger tooth" and "V-band," along with three- and four-white-band patterns, sit as named sub-grades within this same general framework. Grade affects price and breeding value, not care difficulty — a C-grade and an SSS-grade Crystal Red want identical water.

Breeding and genetics

Within Caridina cantonensis, Crystal Red, Crystal Black, and most Taiwan Bee lines are the same species complex and will cross-breed freely, usually passing some degree of white patterning to the offspring regardless of which parent line contributed it. The practical complication for breeders is genetic, not reproductive: the black color gene is dominant over red, so two red parents can still produce black, two-tone, or unpatterned offspring depending on which recessive genes they're each carrying under the surface. Keeping a colony breeding true to a specific color and grade takes several generations of selective breeding — commonly called maintaining a "Pure Red Line" (or the equivalent for black or other color lines) — and culling offspring that don't match, rather than any special feeding or water trick. If you're starting a Caridina colony purely to keep shrimp rather than to breed a specific grade, this mostly explains why offspring from a mixed-source starter group often look less uniform than the adults you bought.

Tank mates

The same caution that applies to Neocaridina applies more strongly here: adult Caridina are small, and shrimplets are smaller still, so most fish marketed as "peaceful community fish" will still eat them given the chance. Caridina's narrower, more easily disrupted water parameters also rule out many fish that would otherwise be considered gentle tank mates, simply because those fish need harder or more neutral water than a Caridina tank is set up to provide. A species-only Caridina tank remains the most reliable way to build and keep a breeding colony, especially for higher grades where losing even a few shrimp to predation is a real setback. See our freshwater fish compatibility guide if you're weighing tank mates anyway, and cross-check any candidate fish against both predation risk and water-parameter overlap before adding it.

Common mistakes that crash a Caridina colony

  • Starting from tap water instead of RO/remineralized water. San Diego tap runs hard and alkaline; trying to soften it down with chemicals alone tends to produce unstable swings rather than genuinely soft water.
  • Using an inert substrate with no buffering. Without an active substrate holding pH down, a near-zero-KH tank has nothing to resist swings from water changes, CO2, or even evaporation and top-offs.
  • Treating Neocaridina care advice as a drop-in substitute. The genera look similar and share feeding and molting basics, but Neocaridina's wider tolerance doesn't transfer — a tank tuned for cherry shrimp is often too hard and too alkaline for Caridina.
  • Large, infrequent water changes "to catch up." A big correction after weeks of neglect is a bigger shock than the neglect itself; smaller, planned changes matched to the tank's drift rate are safer than reactive ones.
  • Copper exposure. As with Neocaridina, copper from medications or fertilizers is lethal to Caridina at levels that don't visibly affect fish — see the copper warning in our Neocaridina guide, which applies identically here.
Are Caridina shrimp harder to keep than Neocaridina?

Sources disagree. Shrimp Science rates Crystal Red Shrimp difficulty as "Medium" and stresses their narrow KH tolerance, while The Shrimp Farm calls them "relatively easy to keep" once water is dialed in. Both agree the setup work (active substrate, RO water, stable low KH) happens before the shrimp go in, not after.

What water parameters do Caridina shrimp need?

Commonly cited ranges are pH 5.5-7.0, GH 4-6, KH 0-2, and TDS 100-180 ppm, cooler and softer than Neocaridina. Sources vary on the exact ceiling: one widely used reference allows KH up to 4 and TDS up to 300, so treat the lower end of any range as the safer target rather than chasing one exact number.

Why do Caridina shrimp need active substrate?

Because the water itself is barely buffered (KH near 0), an active (aquasoil) substrate does the job of holding pH stable and slightly acidic that KH would normally do. Without it, pH in a near-zero-KH tank can swing with every water change or CO2 fluctuation.

What do shrimp grades like S, SS, and SSS mean?

They describe how much white versus red coverage a Crystal Red Shrimp shows and how clean the pattern is, running roughly C (mostly red, thin white stripes) up through B, A, S, SS (Hinomaru, a solid red dot), to SSS (Mosura, nearly all-white with a small red head marking). Grade affects price and breeding value, not hardiness.

Can I keep Caridina and Neocaridina shrimp in the same tank?

They won't interbreed since they're different genera, but their water needs don't overlap well: Neocaridina tolerates harder, more alkaline water than most Caridina want. A tank set up for one is rarely the right water for the other, so keeping them separate is the more reliable route to a stable colony of either.

Why didn't my red Crystal Red shrimp breed true red offspring?

The black color gene is dominant over red in Caridina cantonensis, so two red parents can still throw black or two-tone offspring depending on what recessive genes they carry. Breeders maintain a "Pure Red Line" over several generations specifically to suppress this and keep a colony breeding true.
