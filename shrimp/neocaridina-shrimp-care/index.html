Neocaridina Shrimp Care: A Complete Guide

By Pet Zone SD Team · Updated September 22, 2026 · 11 min read

Neocaridina davidi is the shrimp species most keepers mean when they say "cherry shrimp," and it's also sold under color-line names like red cherry, yellow fire, blue dream, and rili. It's the dwarf shrimp species to start with. Not because it's foolproof, but because it forgives a wider range of everyday water conditions than its more delicate cousins, while still rewarding the same things every shrimp tank needs: a mature tank, stable water, and patience. This guide covers what actually matters for keeping Neocaridina alive, molting successfully, and breeding on their own.

Why Neocaridina, and not Caridina

Freshwater dwarf shrimp in the hobby split into two genera: Neocaridina and Caridina. Caridina species (crystal red/black shrimp, Taiwan bees, tiger shrimp, and most of the "high-grade" ornamental lines) generally need soft, more acidic water held within a narrow band, and they're considerably less tolerant of parameter drift. Neocaridina davidi, by contrast, is native to a range of slow-moving lakes, ponds, and streams across Taiwan, China, Korea, and Vietnam and has adapted to a broader spread of conditions in the wild, which is a large part of why it tolerates a broader spread in captivity too (Wikipedia: Neocaridina davidi). That's the whole case for starting here: more margin for error while you learn to read a shrimp tank, not zero margin for error.

"Tolerant" is relative, though. Neocaridina still die in large numbers in beginner tanks, almost always from the same handful of preventable causes covered below — not from being a naturally fragile species.

Ideal water parameters

Neocaridina do well across a fairly wide band, but "wide" doesn't mean "unstable is fine." A tank that sits steadily at the edge of their tolerance is safer than one that swings through the middle of it. Commonly cited target ranges from shrimp-specific sources are:

ParameterTarget range
Temperature68–78°F, with 68–75°F often cited as ideal for steady breeding
pH6.5–8.0, with 7.0–7.6 considered ideal
GH (general hardness)6–12 dGH, with 9–11 cited as ideal for shell and molt health
KH (carbonate hardness)3–8 dKH, with 4–6 cited as ideal for pH stability
Ammonia / nitrite0 ppm, always

These ranges are drawn from shrimp-keeping references including The Shrimp Farm's Neocaridina care guide and The Aquarium Adviser's shrimp water parameter guide. GH matters more than most beginners expect: it's the calcium and magnesium supply shrimp draw on to rebuild their exoskeleton after every molt, and chronically low GH is a common, under-diagnosed cause of failed molts.

The single biggest parameter mistake isn't picking the wrong number — it's changing the number quickly. A large water change with source water that differs from tank water in pH or GH can shock a whole colony at once, because shrimp have limited ability to osmoregulate against a sudden shift. Change water in smaller, more frequent increments (10–15% weekly rather than 50% monthly) and match temperature and, where practical, hardness before adding new water.

Copper is lethal to shrimp at concentrations that barely register with fish. Invertebrates are far more copper-sensitive than fish, and standard advice for reef and freshwater systems alike is to remove invertebrates before any copper-based medication and not return them until copper is back to 0.01 mg/L or less, ideally zero (University of Florida IFAS Extension, copper toxicity in aquatic systems). Read the label on every fish medication, and check plant fertilizers too — some contain trace copper as a micronutrient, which is fine for fish-only tanks but not for shrimp.

Start with a mature tank, not a new one

Neocaridina do not belong in a brand-new setup. Even trace ammonia is a problem for them, and unlike many fish they show little to no warning before it kills them. If you haven't cycled a tank before, read our full walkthrough on how to cycle a freshwater aquarium first — the short version for shrimp is that 0 ppm ammonia and nitrite is the minimum bar, and it isn't the whole bar.

Shrimp also need something a fish-only cycled tank doesn't necessarily have yet: biofilm. That's the thin living layer of bacteria, microorganisms, and detritus that colonizes hardscape, glass, and substrate over time, and it makes up a large part of a healthy shrimp colony's actual diet. Shrimp-specific guidance generally recommends letting a tank cycle and then mature for an additional few weeks beyond that — commonly cited as four to six weeks total from setup — so biofilm has time to establish before shrimp go in (Shrimp Science, tank cycling). A tank that hit 0/0 on ammonia and nitrite last week but still looks sterile and bare on every hard surface is technically cycled and still a bad idea for shrimp. If you're planning a planted shrimp tank from scratch, our guide to a beginner planted tank covers substrate and plant choices that also help biofilm and algae establish faster.

Molting and why molt safety matters

Shrimp grow by molting: shedding their entire exoskeleton and hardening a new, larger one underneath. Juveniles molt roughly every one to two weeks while growing quickly; adults slow to roughly every three to four weeks (Shrimp Science, molting and common problems). It's a genuinely dangerous window for the animal — for a day or two after molting the new shell is soft, the shrimp is defenseless, and it will typically hide rather than forage.

A few practical rules follow directly from that:

  • Don't do a large water change right after a mass molt. Freshly molted shrimp are especially vulnerable to the osmotic stress of shifting water chemistry.
  • Leave shed exoskeletons in the tank. Shrimp eat their own molts to recover calcium for the next shell, and pulling molts out on sight removes a nutrient source for no benefit. Remove one only if you suspect it's harboring a parasite or bacterial issue, otherwise let it get eaten or break down.
  • Keep GH in range. Low general hardness starves shrimp of the calcium and magnesium a new shell needs, and is a common contributor to molting failures.
  • Watch for "white ring of death." A visible white or opaque ring around a shrimp's body means the old shell split in the wrong place and didn't fully separate — usually fatal, and usually a sign of a mineral deficiency or stress during the molt rather than a random event.

A shrimp lying on its side, twitching, or apparently stuck half in and half out of its old shell is in trouble; manual intervention with tweezers is a last resort that often does more harm than good, so it's better to prevent the underlying parameter or calcium problem than to try to fix a stuck molt in progress.

Feeding

Biofilm and algae should be the bulk of a Neocaridina colony's diet in an established tank, not prepared food. That's part of why algae isn't purely a nuisance in a shrimp tank — a tank with some algae growth is a tank with food in it. If you're dealing with an algae outbreak you'd rather not have, our guide to understanding aquarium algae covers the common types and what actually controls them without stripping the tank bare.

Supplemental feeding should be light and infrequent: a few shrimp-safe pellet or wafer pieces, or a pinch of powdered food for a colony with babies, two to three times a week is enough for a modestly stocked tank. Feed only what a colony will clear within a couple of hours, and don't add more just because you don't see shrimp swarming it immediately — shrimp graze rather than rush food the way many fish do. Overfeeding is a more common and more damaging mistake than underfeeding: uneaten food rots, spikes ammonia, and undoes the stability the rest of this guide is built around.

Breeding basics

In a stable, mature tank, Neocaridina breed on their own without any intervention. A few terms worth knowing:

  • Saddled: a visible patch of color (often yellow or green) across a mature female's back, which is developing eggs still inside her ovaries, signaling she's approaching readiness to mate.
  • Berried: a female carrying fertilized eggs tucked under her tail, held against her swimmerettes. A clutch is commonly in the range of 20–30 eggs, and eggs typically take roughly two to three weeks to hatch depending on temperature (Wikipedia: Neocaridina davidi).

Neocaridina hatch as fully formed miniature shrimp rather than free-swimming larvae, which is a large part of why they're so much easier to breed than Caridina species — there's no separate planktonic larval stage requiring special feeding or brackish water. Once a female is berried, the main job is to leave her alone: don't move her, avoid disturbing the substrate she's hiding in, and keep water changes small and gradual. Warmer water within the ideal range tends to encourage breeding activity, and a colony in good conditions will simply keep going once established.

Color lines and morph-specific notes

Red cherry, yellow fire/neon, blue dream, orange sakura, and rili shrimp are all the same species, Neocaridina davidi, selectively bred for color rather than adapted for different water conditions. Care and parameters are identical across color lines. The one practical wrinkle is genetics, not husbandry: different color morphs will interbreed freely, and offspring from mixed-color tanks tend to revert toward duller, more "wild type" brownish coloration over a few generations. If keeping a specific color line true matters to you, keep that line in its own tank rather than mixing it with other Neocaridina colors.

Tank mates: fewer options than most beginners expect

Shrimp-safe stocking is narrower than "peaceful community fish" lists usually suggest, because plenty of fish marketed as beginner-friendly will happily eat shrimp, especially small or freshly molted ones. Adult Neocaridina are large enough and quick enough to coexist with some small, genuinely peaceful fish; shrimplets rarely survive in a tank with any fish that has a mouth big enough to fit them.

Reasonable tank mates tend to be small, non-predatory species such as small peaceful tetras kept in a proper school, small rasboras, and nerite or similar algae-grazing snails that don't compete for the same food or bother shrimp. Species to avoid include most cichlids, bettas kept with anything but the boldest adult shrimp (results are inconsistent and often bad), larger barbs, and gouramis — all are known shrimp predators regardless of how they're marketed. If you want a genuinely predictable outcome, a shrimp-only tank remains the safest option, and it's also the easiest way to build a breeding colony without predation loss. For picking fish tank mates in general, our guides to beginner freshwater fish and freshwater fish compatibility go into stocking choices in more depth — just cross-check any fish you're considering against "will eat shrimp" before assuming a beginner-friendly fish is shrimp-friendly too.

Common causes of shrimp die-offs

When an established-looking shrimp colony crashes overnight, it's almost never mysterious. The recurring causes, in roughly the order keepers run into them, are:

  • Copper. From a fish medication, a plant fertilizer, or even old copper plumbing feeding tap water. Lethal to shrimp at levels that don't visibly affect fish.
  • Adding shrimp to an immature or freshly cycled tank. "0 ammonia, 0 nitrite" on a test kit isn't the same as a tank with established biofilm and truly stable chemistry.
  • Sudden pH, GH, or KH swings — most often from a large water change using source water that differs meaningfully from tank water, or from adding buffering substrate or minerals too aggressively.
  • Ammonia spikes from overfeeding, overstocking, or a filter that lost its bacterial colony (after a filter clean in tap water, a power outage, or antibiotic use).
  • General-purpose fish medications beyond copper — many broad-spectrum treatments are formulated without invertebrates in mind and carry no shrimp-safety guidance on the label at all. When in doubt, remove shrimp before treating a tank for fish disease.

Every one of these is a stability failure, not evidence that Neocaridina are secretly fragile. A colony that's been through a proper cycle, gets small and infrequent water changes, and never sees copper or aggressive dosing tends to be genuinely low-maintenance — which is the reputation the species earns when it's set up correctly in the first place.

Are Neocaridina shrimp easy to keep?

Yes, relative to most other dwarf shrimp. They tolerate a wider range of pH, hardness, and temperature than Caridina species, but they still need a mature, cycled tank and stable water — not just "easy."

Why do my shrimp keep dying for no obvious reason?

The usual causes are a tank that wasn't fully cycled before shrimp went in, a sudden swing in pH or GH from a water change, trace copper from fertilizer or medication, or an ammonia spike. Shrimp show almost no warning signs before any of these kill them.

Do I need to feed my shrimp every day?

No. In an established tank, biofilm and algae cover most of their diet. A few pinhead-sized pieces of food two to three times a week is enough for a small colony; uneaten food fouls the water faster than it feeds anyone.

Is copper really that dangerous to shrimp?

Yes. Shrimp and other invertebrates are far more sensitive to copper than fish are, and many general fish medications and some plant fertilizers contain it. Always check the label before dosing a tank that holds shrimp.

Can I keep Neocaridina shrimp with fish?

Only with small, peaceful, non-predatory species, and adult shrimp fare better than babies. Many popular "beginner" fish will eat shrimp, especially newly molted or juvenile ones.
