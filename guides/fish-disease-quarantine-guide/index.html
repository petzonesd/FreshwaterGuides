Fish Disease & Quarantine Guide: Symptoms to Treatment

By Pet Zone SD TeamUpdated October 10, 202612 min read

Most disease outbreaks in home aquariums don't start with bad luck — they start with a new fish, plant, or piece of used decor going straight into an established tank with no observation period first. A fish can look completely healthy in a bag and still be carrying ich, an internal parasite, or a bacterial infection that stress brings out days later, by which point it's shared water with every other fish you own. Quarantine is the one habit that catches this before it becomes a tank-wide problem, and most of the diseases below are far easier to treat in a bare 10-gallon tank with one sick fish than in a stocked display with no way to remove the fish you're treating.

This guide covers the diseases you're most likely to actually encounter, how to tell them apart by what you're looking at, and how to run a quarantine period that catches problems before they reach your main tank. If your tank itself is brand new, start with how to cycle a freshwater aquarium first — an unstable nitrogen cycle is a major driver of stress-related disease in new setups.

Why quarantine comes before everything else

A fish that just survived a bag, a car ride, and a temperature swing is stressed, and stress is what turns a parasite or bacteria it was already carrying into a visible outbreak. That's true even for fish from a careful, reputable store — it isn't about where you bought the fish, it's about the fact that shipping and rehoming stress every fish, every time. A two-to-four week isolation period in a separate tank gives symptoms time to show up somewhere other than your main display, where you can still easily net, dose, or euthanize a sick fish without tearing apart an aquascape or exposing fish you can't afford to lose.

The same logic applies to plants and used equipment, not just livestock. Live plants can arrive carrying pest snail eggs, hydra, or planaria, and used decor, substrate, or filter media can carry bacteria or fungal spores from a previous tank. A plant doesn't need a heater or feeding during its own one-to-two-week hold, but skipping that hold is one of the most common ways hobbyists introduce a problem they never traced back to the actual source.

Setting up a quarantine tank that actually works

A quarantine (QT) tank doesn't need to be fancy, and in most cases it shouldn't be. A bare-bottom 10-20 gallon tank is easier to keep clean and makes a sick fish far easier to spot and net than substrate and plants would. Run a sponge filter seeded with bacteria from an established tank (or a cycled filter held over specifically for this purpose) so you're not fighting a second nitrogen cycle on top of whatever the fish brought with it, add a heater matched to the species, and keep the lighting dim — bright light adds stress and, for a parasite like ich or velvet, can actually accelerate its life cycle.

  • Acclimate slowly. Float or drip-acclimate for temperature, then net the fish into the QT tank rather than pouring in bag water, and dim or turn the lights off for the first few hours.
  • Hold off on food for a day or two, then feed lightly. A stressed fish that isn't eating yet is normal for the first 24-48 hours; a fish still refusing food after a week is a warning sign.
  • Test ammonia and nitrite daily for the first week, especially if the QT filter isn't fully established — see our water parameters guide for what to test and what the numbers mean.
  • Observe at least once or twice a day. Watch for flashing (scratching against surfaces), clamped fins, rapid breathing, and appetite — most of the diseases below show behavioral signs before they show visible ones.
  • Medicate only if you see symptoms, unless the fish is wild-caught or came from a source known to carry parasites. Preventive medication stresses a healthy fish for no benefit and can wipe out the QT tank's own bacteria.

Hold healthy-looking fish for two weeks at an absolute minimum if they already spent time looking fine in a store tank; three to four weeks is the safer, more standard window, and wild-caught or especially delicate species are often held one to two months by experienced keepers.

Ich (white spot disease): the most common new-fish problem

Ich is caused by a ciliate parasite, Ichthyophthirius multifiliis, and it's the disease most hobbyists encounter first because it's so common in the trade. The visible white dots are pustules the parasite forms under the skin; it then drops off into the tank, encysts on a surface, and divides into anywhere from 100 to over 1,000 free-swimming tomites that go find new fish to infect (Wikivet, White Spot (Ich)). That life cycle is strongly temperature-dependent — it runs noticeably faster as water warms toward the mid-70s°F, which is also why ich outbreaks often follow a seasonal temperature swing or a new fish shipped in cold weather.

Early signs are often behavioral before they're visible: fish flashing against decor, clustering near filter outlets, or looking generally irritated before the first white dots appear on fins or body. Standard treatment raises temperature a few degrees (speeding the parasite's cycle into its more vulnerable free-swimming stage) alongside a formalin, malachite green, or salt-based medication over several days to a week — and this is exactly the kind of multi-day treatment that's far more manageable in an empty 10-gallon QT tank than in a planted display full of fish and expensive livestock.

Fin rot: usually a water-quality problem wearing a disease costume

Fin rot shows up as fraying, shortening fin edges, sometimes with a thin white or milky line marking where healthy tissue ends. It's caused by opportunistic bacteria that take hold once fin tissue is already compromised — by nipping tankmates, rough decor, or most commonly, water that's let ammonia, nitrite, or nitrate run higher than it should. Mild fin rot on an otherwise active, eating fish often resolves simply by fixing the water quality behind it and keeping up with water changes; see our maintenance schedule for the routine that prevents it from starting in the first place.

Severe fin rot — rot that's reached the body, fins with a red or bloody base, or a fish that's stopped eating — needs an antibacterial medication on top of the water-quality fix, since the infection has outpaced what clean water alone will reverse.

Columnaris: fast-moving, often mistaken for fungus

Columnaris (Flavobacterium columnare) causes white, grey, or yellow cottony-looking patches, often starting on the mouth, back, or fin edges, and it's frequently misdiagnosed as a fungal infection because of that cotton-like appearance. The distinction matters: columnaris is bacterial and spreads fast enough to be fatal within 24 to 72 hours in acute cases, and an antifungal medication does nothing for it. The bacteria exist naturally in most aquarium water and become dangerous specifically when fish are stressed, injured, or kept in poor conditions — overcrowding, ammonia or nitrite, and sudden temperature swings are the usual triggers.

Treatment means moving fast: isolate the fish if possible, correct ammonia, nitrite, and temperature immediately, increase aeration since columnaris often affects the gills, and use an antibacterial medication labeled effective against Flavobacterium rather than a general antifungal product.

Fungus (Saprolegnia): a real fungus, and usually a secondary problem

True fungal infections look similar to columnaris at a glance — cottony white or grey growth — but behave differently. Saprolegnia and related fungi are opportunists that colonize tissue that's already dead or damaged: an open wound, an unfertilized egg, or a patch of skin lost to an earlier injury. Unlike columnaris, fungus tends to stay fixed on that one spot rather than racing across the body within a day. Treatment combines an antifungal medication with addressing whatever caused the original damage — a fungal patch that keeps returning on the same spot usually means the underlying injury or water-quality issue was never actually fixed.

Velvet (gold dust disease): subtle until it's advanced

Velvet gets its name from the fine gold or rust-colored dusting it leaves across the body, most visible when a flashlight is shone across the fish in a dark room. It's caused by a photosynthetic parasite in the Oodinium/Piscinoodinium group, and that photosynthetic dependence is the key to treating it: the free-swimming stage needs light to fuel its search for a host, so several days of total darkness measurably slows the outbreak (Wikipedia, Velvet (fish disease)). Early signs are flashing, clamped fins, and lethargy, often before the dusting is obvious — and because velvet frequently starts on the gills, a fish that's visibly dusted on the skin may already be struggling to breathe.

Standard treatment pairs that darkness period with aquarium salt or a copper-based medication; some species, including anchor catfish and other scaleless fish, tolerate salt poorly, so confirm a species' salt tolerance before dosing the full amount.

Dropsy: a symptom, not a disease you can name and cure

Dropsy isn't a single disease — it's visible fluid buildup inside a fish's body, caused by kidneys or gills that can no longer regulate fluid balance. The underlying trigger is most often a bacterial infection, sometimes combined with chronic poor water quality, poor nutrition, or in rarer cases a severe parasite load, liver disease, or a tumor. The telltale "pinecone" look — scales sticking outward from a swollen body, along with bulging eyes and a fish struggling to close its mouth — usually means the underlying problem has already been building for weeks.

Treatment combines antibiotics with immaculate water and supportive care, and some fish improve over days to weeks if caught early. Once the pinecone appearance is pronounced, though, organ damage is often severe enough that the prognosis is poor regardless of treatment — which is one more reason the disease table above and a real quarantine period matter: catching the bacterial infection behind dropsy before it reaches that stage is far more effective than treating dropsy itself.

When in doubt between columnaris and fungus, treat for columnaris first. Misreading a fast-spreading bacterial infection as "just fungus" and reaching for an antifungal is one of the more common and more costly mistakes in home disease treatment — the delay while the wrong medication does nothing is exactly what columnaris uses to outrun you.

Red flags that need isolation right now, not a wait-and-see

Most of what's above is manageable with a proper quarantine habit and prompt, correct treatment. Act immediately — isolate the fish and start treatment the same day — if you see rapid or labored breathing, a cottony patch that's visibly larger than it was a few hours earlier, more than one fish showing symptoms at once, or a fish that stops eating and sits at the bottom. Those are the signs that separate a contained, treatable case from an outbreak that reaches the rest of the tank before you've finished reading a care sheet.

Do I really need to quarantine every new fish, even from a trusted store?

Yes. A fish can carry ich, internal parasites, or a low-grade bacterial infection and still look completely normal in a bag or a store tank — stress from shipping and a new tank is often what triggers visible symptoms days later. Quarantine catches that before it reaches a display tank with fish you can't easily remove.

How long should a quarantine period actually last?

Two weeks is a reasonable minimum for fish that already spent time healthy in a store tank. Three to four weeks is safer and standard advice for most hobbyists, and wild-caught or delicate species are often held one to two months. Longer is always safer than shorter.

Can disease come in on plants or decor, not just fish?

Yes. Live plants can carry parasite eggs, hydra, or pest snails, and used decor or substrate can carry bacteria or fungal spores. A plant-only quarantine doesn't need a heater or feeding, but a one- to two-week observation and a rinse before planting is still worth doing.

Is dropsy a disease I can cure?

Dropsy isn't a disease itself — it's a symptom of fluid buildup from organ failure, usually caused by an underlying bacterial infection, chronic poor water quality, or in rare cases a parasite or tumor. Early cases sometimes resolve with antibiotics and pristine water, but once the pinecone-scale look is pronounced, the prognosis is poor.

Why do people use aquarium salt for ich and velvet but not other diseases?

Ich and velvet are both parasites with a free-swimming life stage outside the fish, and that stage is more vulnerable to salt than the fish's own cells are. Bacterial diseases like columnaris and fin rot need an antibacterial medication instead — salt alone won't touch them, and it won't help a fungal infection either.

How can I tell a fungal infection apart from columnaris?

True fungus (Saprolegnia) looks like cottony white or grey tufts growing from one specific spot, almost always an existing wound, dead tissue, or an unhatched egg — and it stays put. Columnaris is bacterial, spreads much faster across the body or fins within a day or two, and often shows yellow or grey edges rather than pure white cotton. When in doubt, treat with an antibacterial first, since misidentifying columnaris as fungus and reaching for an antifungal is the more common and more costly mistake.
