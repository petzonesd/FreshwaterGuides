Understanding Aquarium Algae (Diagnose Before You Treat)

By Pet Zone SD Team · Updated September 22, 2026 · 11 min read

Every established aquarium has algae in it. The question isn't whether algae is present, it's whether it's under control. When it isn't, the instinct is to reach for an algaecide, a UV sterilizer, or a bag of algae-eating shrimp. Sometimes those help. But algae itself is rarely the actual problem — it's a visible symptom of an imbalance somewhere in the tank: too much light for the nutrients available, too little CO2 for the light you're running, or organic waste building up faster than your maintenance routine removes it. Treat the visible algae without touching the underlying cause, and it comes back, often within days.

This guide walks through the algae types you're most likely to see in a freshwater tank, what each one is usually telling you about your setup, and the fix that actually holds. If your tank is still cycling, start with how to cycle a freshwater aquarium first — an unstable nitrogen cycle is behind a lot of "mystery" algae in new tanks.

Algae is a symptom, not a standalone problem

Algae are simple photosynthetic organisms (cyanobacteria aren't even true algae — more on that below). Like any photosynthetic life, they need three things to grow: light, a carbon source, and dissolved nutrients, chiefly nitrogen and phosphorus. Your aquarium already supplies all three, continuously, through fish waste, uneaten food, tap water, and whatever lighting schedule you're running. Algae spores are also always present in an established tank; they don't need to be introduced. So the question is never "how do I keep algae spores out" — it's "why do current conditions favor algae growing over my plants, or over nothing at all."

Aqueon's aquarium care guidance frames it plainly: nutrient buildup from fish waste and uneaten food, combined with excess light, is what triggers algae blooms in the first place — not a single bad ingredient, but the balance between them (Aqueon, Prevention and Control of Nuisance Algae). A tank with strong plant growth, disciplined feeding, and a sensible photoperiod rarely has a persistent algae problem, because the plants are consuming the same nutrients and light the algae would otherwise use. That's also why a heavily planted tank tends to run cleaner than a bare one with identical fish stocking — see your first planted tank for how to set that balance up from the start.

Products marketed as instant algae fixes — algaecides, chemical clarifiers, "algae-safe" light bulbs — can knock back what's visible. None of them change light duration, nutrient load, or CO2 stability. Unless you also address the cause, you're re-buying the same product every few weeks.

Identify what you're actually looking at

"Algae" isn't one organism, and different types point to different root causes. A quick visual ID is the first real step in fixing it — treating black beard algae like it's diatoms, or green water like it's green spot algae, wastes time and money.

Green water: a free-floating algae bloom

Green water isn't algae growing on a surface — it's single-celled algae (commonly Chlorella, Scenedesmus, or similar genera) suspended throughout the water column, which is why the whole tank turns cloudy green almost overnight (Wikipedia, Freshwater aquarium algae). It's typically triggered by a spike in available light — direct sun hitting the tank, a light left on too long, or a new higher-output fixture — landing on top of nutrients that were already elevated from feeding or a missed water change (Aqueon, Green Water: Causes and Cures).

Water changes alone usually won't clear it, because you're diluting a bloom that regrows as fast as you remove it. A multi-day blackout (lights off, tank covered) starves the bloom of light and generally clears it within three to five days. A UV sterilizer is the more reliable fix for a tank that keeps relapsing — it kills suspended algae cells as water passes through, without touching the nutrients feeding it. Either way, if you don't also reduce photoperiod or nutrient load afterward, it comes back.

Green spot algae: usually a sign you're running lean

Green spot algae shows up as small, hard, circular green dots that adhere tightly to glass, slow-growing leaves, and hardscape — it doesn't wipe off as easily as diatoms do. Counterintuitively, GSA is often a sign of a tank that's short on CO2 and phosphate relative to the light it's getting, not a sign of excess nutrients. Hobbyist references built around planted-tank chemistry consistently describe it this way: plenty of light, but not enough carbon or phosphate for plants (or other algae) to out-compete it (2Hr Aquarist, How to Control Green Spot Algae). The practical fix is usually to raise CO2 stability and confirm phosphate isn't being stripped to zero (common in tanks running aggressive phosphate-removing media), rather than reducing light further.

Brown algae (diatoms): the new-tank algae that usually fixes itself

If your tank is under two or three months old and a soft brown dust has coated the glass and slower-growing plant leaves, you're almost certainly looking at diatoms. They're extremely common in new setups because new substrate, glass, and even tap water release silicates, and a young biofilter hasn't yet stabilized ammonia and nitrite — both conditions diatoms exploit (Green Aqua, Silicate in the Water: Brown Algae/Diatoms in the Aquarium).

The good news: diatoms usually resolve on their own as the tank matures, typically within two to six weeks, once the filter's bacterial colony is established and silicate levels settle. Wiping the glass and doing normal water changes speeds it along; otocinclus catfish and nerite snails will graze it down in the meantime. What you shouldn't do is panic-dose a silicate remover or algaecide in week two — you'll likely spend money treating something that was already on its way out.

Black beard / brush algae: the CO2-instability algae

Black beard algae (BBA) forms dense, dark grey-to-black tufts, often on leaf edges, driftwood, and — distinctively — in areas of strong water flow like filter outlets and CO2 diffusers. It's one of the more stubborn algae types to eliminate once established.

The strongest evidence points to CO2 instability, not just low CO2, as the trigger. In CO2-injected planted tanks, fluctuating CO2 levels appear to stress plants enough that they leak metabolites, and those leaks act as a signal for BBA spores to attach and grow — which is also why BBA tends to appear first on older, slower-growing leaves and leaf edges rather than new growth (2Hr Aquarist, How to Control BBA). High flow doesn't cause BBA on its own, but it does concentrate spores and organic debris in exactly the spots where that plant stress is happening.

The fix is consistency, not intensity: stable CO2 delivery on a timer that comes on well before lights and off shortly after, steady flow without dead spots, and removing the worst-affected leaves rather than trying to scrub established BBA clean. Liquid carbon products (glutaraldehyde-based) can suppress it directly, but they're treating the symptom — a tank running stable CO2 rarely develops BBA in the first place.

Green hair / thread algae: a nutrient-ratio problem

Fine green filaments that tangle around plant stems and hardscape, ranging from short fuzz to long trailing threads, usually point to a nutrient imbalance rather than a straightforward excess or deficiency. It's frequently tied to too much light relative to available CO2, or an iron or nitrate level that's high relative to what the plants in the tank can actually use — in other words, more of an input than the plants can absorb, leaving the surplus for algae (Wikipedia, Freshwater aquarium algae). It's also common in newly set up planted tanks before plants have filled in and started competing effectively, and tends to fade on its own as the tank matures — see your first planted tank for planting density that shortens that window.

Manual removal (twirling it out with a toothbrush or your fingers) combined with dialing back photoperiod and confirming dosing isn't overshooting your plant mass is the standard fix. Amano shrimp are genuinely effective against hair algae specifically, more so than against most other types.

Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria): not actually algae

Despite the name, blue-green algae isn't algae at all — it's cyanobacteria, a photosynthetic bacteria (AquariumScience.org, Blue-Green Algae). It shows up as a slimy sheet, usually blue-green or dark green, sometimes almost black, that peels off surfaces in one piece and often carries a distinct musty or "swampy" smell. Typical drivers are a combination of poor water movement, warm water, and organic buildup — dead spots with low oxygen and accumulated waste are exactly where it thrives.

This is also why erythromycin-based "algae" treatments are a last resort, not a first move: erythromycin is an antibiotic, and it works on cyanobacteria specifically because cyanobacteria is bacteria, not because it's an algaecide. It does nothing for true algae, it can knock back the beneficial bacteria in your filter along with the cyanobacteria (risking an ammonia or nitrite spike), and it only offers temporary relief if the underlying flow and waste problem isn't fixed (AquariumScience.org, Blue-Green Algae). The more durable fix is manual removal with a siphon (not just wiping — spread cyanobacteria easily), increasing circulation to eliminate dead spots, a temporary blackout similar to the green water approach, and tightening up gravel vacuuming and feeding so organic waste stops accumulating.

Prevention: the four levers that actually matter

Every algae type above traces back to the same handful of variables. Get these right and most algae problems become occasional and minor instead of chronic.

  • Photoperiod discipline. Most freshwater tanks do well with 6-8 hours of light for a fish-only tank, or 8-10 hours for a planted tank, on a timer so it's consistent day to day. Longer photoperiods and direct sunlight are two of the most common algae triggers, and they're also the easiest to fix — no product required.
  • Nutrient balance, not nutrient elimination. Plants need nitrogen, phosphorus, and trace elements too; starving your tank of everything just shifts the problem toward GSA and diatoms instead of hair algae. The goal is nutrients in proportion to your light and CO2, not zero.
  • Water change consistency. Aqueon's guidance targets nitrate under 10 ppm and phosphate under 0.5 ppm through regular partial water changes (Aqueon, Prevention and Control of Nuisance Algae). A consistent 10-20% weekly change does more for algae prevention than almost any product on the shelf, because it caps how high nutrients can climb between now and next week.
  • Flow and dead-spot elimination. Stagnant corners, behind decorations, under low-flow filter placement, accumulate detritus and low-oxygen water — prime cyanobacteria and BBA territory. Rearranging flow so the whole tank turns over, not just the area right in front of the filter, closes off a lot of algae's favorite real estate.

If you're not sure which lever is off, test your water before changing anything. A tank with 40 ppm nitrate and 12 hours of light needs a different fix than one with near-zero nitrate and unstable CO2 — and they can look like similar algae on the glass.

The honest role of a cleanup crew

Amano shrimp, nerite snails, otocinclus catfish, and similar livestock genuinely eat algae, and a well-chosen cleanup crew makes a real difference in day-to-day appearance. They're particularly effective against diatoms and green hair algae, less so against BBA and cyanobacteria, which many species avoid eating altogether.

What a cleanup crew doesn't do is fix light, nutrients, CO2, or flow. Adding shrimp to a tank with a 14-hour photoperiod and no water change routine buys you a temporary improvement at best — the algae's growth rate hasn't changed, only its removal rate has, and the crew can fall behind the moment feeding gets generous or a bulb gets brighter. Think of a cleanup crew as ongoing maintenance help layered on top of a corrected root cause, not a substitute for one. When to actually worry

A small amount of algae on old glass, a few green spots that show up between wipe-downs, or a light dusting of diatoms in a tank under two months old are normal biology, not signs of failure. What's worth acting on is algae that's spreading week over week, coating plant leaves faster than they can grow past it, or recurring within days of every manual cleaning — that pattern means one of the four levers above is genuinely off, and it's worth testing your water and reviewing your photoperiod before buying anything.

Is algae always a sign something is wrong?

Not always. A few green spots on old glass or a light dusting of diatoms in a new tank is normal biology, not a crisis. Persistent, spreading, or recurring algae is the signal worth acting on.

Will a cleanup crew get rid of my algae?

Algae-eating shrimp, snails, and fish reduce the algae you can see, but they don't change light, nutrients, or flow. If the underlying imbalance stays the same, algae regrows faster than a cleanup crew can eat it.

Why did diatoms take over my brand-new tank?

New tanks usually have unstable ammonia, fresh substrate, and glass and tap water that release silicates while the filter's bacteria are still establishing. Diatoms exploit that window and typically fade within a few weeks.

Is blue-green algae actually algae?

No. Despite the name, blue-green algae is cyanobacteria, a photosynthetic bacteria. That's why algae-specific treatments often fail on it and why some hobbyists use erythromycin, which is really an antibacterial, not an algaecide.

Do I need a UV sterilizer or algaecide to fix algae?

Rarely as a first step. Most algae types respond to fixing light, nutrients, CO2, or flow within one to three weeks. UV sterilizers and algaecides can help with specific cases like green water, but they don't address why the algae appeared.
