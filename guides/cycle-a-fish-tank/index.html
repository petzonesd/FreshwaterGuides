How to Cycle a Freshwater Aquarium

Cycling means growing enough beneficial bacteria in your filter and substrate to convert toxic ammonia (from fish waste and uneaten food) into nitrite, then into much safer nitrate. A fishless cycle typically takes 4 to 6 weeks; skipping it is the single most common reason new aquariums lose fish in the first month.

Written by the Pet Zone SD Team · Updated September 2026

What "cycling" actually means

Every aquarium needs a colony of beneficial bacteria living in the filter media, substrate, and on hard surfaces. These bacteria process fish waste in two stages: first they convert ammonia (NH3), which is toxic to fish even in small amounts, into nitrite (NO2-), which is also toxic. A second group of bacteria then converts that nitrite into nitrate (NO3-), which is far less harmful and is removed through regular water changes.

Until that bacteria colony is established, any ammonia your fish produce simply accumulates in the water instead of being broken down. That's what "New Tank Syndrome" is: fish stressed or dying from ammonia and nitrite spikes in a tank that looks perfectly clean.

How long does cycling take?

A fishless cycle using a pure ammonia source usually takes 4 to 6 weeks from a cold start. It can be faster or slower depending on temperature, the ammonia source, and whether you seed the tank with bacteria from an established aquarium.

Fishless cycling, step by step

1. Set up the tank completely

Add substrate, decor, filter, and heater, and run the filter continuously from day one. Set the heater to your target temperature (usually 76-80°F, which speeds up bacterial growth compared to cooler water).

2. Add an ammonia source

Dose pure, additive-free ammonia (no surfactants or perfumes) to reach roughly 2-4 ppm. Test with a liquid reagent kit rather than strips, which are noticeably less accurate at the concentrations that matter here.

3. Test daily and track the numbers

Ammonia will stay elevated for one to two weeks, then start dropping as nitrite rises. Keep dosing ammonia back up to 2-4 ppm each time it drops to zero, so the bacteria colony always has something to process.

4. Wait for nitrite to rise, then fall

Nitrite typically spikes a week or two after ammonia starts dropping, sometimes climbing higher on a test kit's color chart than ammonia ever did. This is normal. A second bacteria colony is establishing itself to consume it.

5. Confirm the cycle is complete

Your tank is cycled when, within 24 hours of dosing ammonia to 2-4 ppm, both ammonia and nitrite read zero and nitrate is measurable. At that point, do a large water change to bring nitrate down before adding any livestock.

Signs your tank is cycled

  • Ammonia drops to 0 ppm within 24 hours of dosing
  • Nitrite drops to 0 ppm within 24 hours of dosing
  • Nitrate is present and measurable (typically 5-40 ppm)
  • Readings stay consistent for at least 2-3 consecutive days
Not sure your test kit readings are accurate? Most local fish stores will check a water sample against a fresh reagent kit at no charge if you bring one in — it's a quick way to rule out a bad or expired test kit before you second-guess your whole cycle.

Common cycling mistakes

The most common error is adding fish too early because ammonia and nitrite both briefly touch zero mid-cycle before spiking again — always confirm zero readings hold for several consecutive days. The second is under-dosing ammonia so the bacteria colony never has enough to grow on, and the third is cleaning the filter media too aggressively once fish are in, which can wipe out the very bacteria doing the work.

Frequently asked questions

Can I speed up cycling a fish tank?

Yes. Adding filter media, gravel, or a sponge filter from an already-established, healthy tank seeds your new tank with beneficial bacteria immediately, which can cut a fishless cycle down to 1-2 weeks instead of 4-6.

Do I need to cycle a tank that has live plants?

Yes, though heavily planted tanks cycle faster because plants absorb some ammonia directly. You should still test water before adding fish rather than assuming plants alone make it safe.

Can I use fish food instead of ammonia to cycle a tank?

You can, but it's slower and messier than dosing pure ammonia directly, since the food has to decompose first to release ammonia. Pure, additive-free ammonia gives you more control over dosing and timing.

Is bottled bacteria enough to cycle a tank on its own?

A quality bottled bacteria product can significantly speed up cycling, but it works best alongside an ammonia source and daily testing, not as a total substitute for the process.

How often should I test water after the tank is cycled?

Test weekly for the first month after adding fish, then every 1-2 weeks once the tank is stable, and always after adding new livestock, changing filtration, or noticing unusual fish behavior.
