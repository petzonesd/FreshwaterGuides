Aquarium Water Change Guide: How Much, How Often, How To

By Pet Zone SD TeamUpdated October 29, 202611 min read

Most community freshwater tanks do well with a 10-25% water change weekly — more and more often for heavily stocked or large-fish tanks, less for lightly stocked, heavily planted ones that pull nutrients out of the water column themselves. There's no single correct number for every tank, because a water change isn't really about the water going in; it's about diluting whatever has built up in the water going out, and how fast that happens depends entirely on your stocking, feeding, and filtration.

The filter's beneficial bacteria handle ammonia and nitrite, converting them step by step into nitrate — but nothing in a normal freshwater setup removes nitrate itself except water changes and, to a smaller degree, live plants. Skip changes long enough and nitrate (and whatever else is accumulating) keeps climbing no matter how healthy your filter is. That's the core reason a cycled, stable tank still needs regular water changes. If you haven't read it yet, our cycling guide covers what the filter actually does and doesn't do.

How much and how often, by tank type

Tank typeTypical scheduleWhy
Standard community tank, moderate stocking10-25% weeklyKeeps nitrate and trace waste from accumulating between changes
Heavily stocked or large/messy fish (cichlids, goldfish, large catfish)20-30%, 1-2x weeklyHigher bioload means waste and nitrate build up faster
Lightly stocked, heavily planted (low-tech or high-tech)10-20%, every 1-2 weeksPlants consume a meaningful share of the nitrate and other nutrients themselves
Shrimp-only tank10-15% weekly, smaller and gentlerShrimp are more sensitive to sudden parameter and TDS swings than most fish
New tank, still cyclingAs needed to keep ammonia/nitrite near zero if fish-in cyclingChanges here are damage control, not routine maintenance — see our cycling guide
Long-neglected tank (old tank syndrome)~25% every day or every other day until parameters normalizeGradual dilution avoids shocking fish that have adapted to the degraded water — see below

Treat this table as a starting range, not a rule. The only reliable way to know if your schedule is enough is to test nitrate a day or two before your next scheduled change — if it's already climbing well past your tank's comfortable range, you're not changing enough water, or often enough. Why a healthy filter still isn't enough on its own

It's a common misconception that a fully cycled tank with a strong filter needs less frequent water changes. The nitrogen cycle converts ammonia to nitrite to nitrate, but that's where it stops in a typical freshwater setup — nitrate itself isn't broken down further without specialized anaerobic conditions most home tanks don't have. The only things pulling it back down are water changes and plant uptake. A tank can be perfectly cycled, with zero ammonia and zero nitrite, and still be quietly accumulating nitrate, dissolved organics, and depleted buffering (KH) between every change. Water changes also replace minerals (general hardness and carbonate hardness) that fish, plants, and the bacteria colony itself use up over time, which plain top-offs for evaporation don't do.

Dechlorinating tap water, and why it's not optional

Municipal tap water is treated with chlorine or chloramine specifically to kill bacteria, and neither additive distinguishes between the bacteria in a water-treatment plant and the beneficial bacteria colonizing your filter, or between those bacteria and your fish's gill tissue. A dechlorinator (water conditioner) neutralizes both, most commonly using sodium thiosulfate to convert them into harmless byproducts. Chlorine alone will slowly evaporate out of standing, aerated water over a day or so, but chloramine doesn't break down that way and needs a conditioner every time (Aquarium Co-Op, How to Treat Tap Water).

One detail that catches people out: a basic dechlorinator only neutralizes the chlorine portion of chloramine, leaving the ammonia it was bound to behind — and that ammonia is toxic on its own. Several conditioner formulations solve this by temporarily binding the freed ammonia for roughly 24 hours, giving the bacteria colony time to process it normally, so check that a conditioner actually covers chloramine rather than chlorine alone if your water utility uses it (most municipal systems in the US now do). If you're on well water that hasn't been chemically treated, you may not need a conditioner at all, though it's worth testing once and checking for heavy metals, since some conditioners help bind those too.

Dechlorinating uses up a small amount of dissolved oxygen in the process of neutralizing chlorine/chloramine. It's rarely an issue on a routine 10-25% change, but it's one more reason a single enormous water change is riskier than the same volume split across a few smaller ones, especially in a tank that's already short on surface agitation.

The gravel-vac technique, step by step

A siphon or gravel vacuum does two jobs at once — removing old water and pulling debris out of the substrate — which is why most water changes are done with one rather than just draining water from the surface.

  • Start the siphon. Submerge the tube at a diagonal with the opening facing up until it fills with water, then raise the far end above the tank rim until flow starts into your bucket (or a hose run to a drain). If the water level is too low for that, mouth-start it and move the end to the bucket quickly.
  • Move decor out of the way first. Waste collects underneath ornaments and hardscape, so shift anything movable before you start vacuuming that spot.
  • Work the substrate in sections. Push the tube into the gravel and let it pull debris up; briefly crimping the hose lets heavier gravel drop back while lighter waste keeps moving. Clean roughly a third of the substrate per change and rotate which third over successive changes, rather than trying to do the whole tank every time.
  • Go lighter over sand. A sand substrate scatters more easily than gravel — hover just above it rather than pushing the tube down into it, or you'll pull up a layer of sand along with the waste.
  • Stop cleanly. Cover the tube opening with your hand before lifting it out, so dirty water in the hose doesn't drain back into the tank.
  • Refill with treated, temperature-matched water. Add the dechlorinator to new water before (or as) it goes in, and get it close to the tank's existing temperature — more on why below.

(Technique sourced from Aquarium Co-Op, How to Use a Gravel Vacuum.) Our maintenance schedule guide covers where a water change fits alongside filter care and glass cleaning in a realistic weekly routine.

Match the temperature, every time

Refill water that's noticeably colder or warmer than the tank can stress fish and, in sensitive species, trigger visible symptoms like clamped fins or erratic swimming after an otherwise routine change. Running the tap to temperature before filling your bucket, or letting treated water sit near the tank briefly, is a small step that removes a surprisingly common cause of "my fish act off after every water change."

Old tank syndrome: when neglect catches up all at once

"Old tank syndrome" describes a tank — sometimes months old, sometimes years established — where water changes have been too small, too infrequent, or skipped outright for long enough that waste quietly climbs to a critical level. The telltale signs are high nitrate, low pH, depleted KH (buffering capacity), and sometimes measurable ammonia or nitrite even in a tank that was cycled fine originally (The Tropical Tank, Old Tank Syndrome). The cruelest part is that the existing fish often look fine: they've gradually adjusted to the slowly worsening water over weeks or months. The problem usually surfaces when a new fish is added to that same water and struggles or dies, because it never had time to adapt the way the established fish did.

The fix is not one big water change. A tank that's drifted that far has fish acclimated to its current, degraded chemistry, and a single large change can swing pH, hardness, and temperature enough to shock them — trading a slow problem for a sudden one. The safer route is small, repeated changes: roughly 25% every day or every other day, retesting nitrate and pH as you go, until parameters settle back into a normal range. A large 50-75% change is sometimes used when a tank is close to an outright crash, but treat that as an emergency measure, not the default fix (Practical Fishkeeping, How to Avoid Old Tank Syndrome). Our glossary has a short explainer on new tank syndrome too — the opposite-timing problem, caused by too little established filtration rather than too little maintenance.

What makes a water change worse instead of better

  • Skipping the dechlorinator "just this once." Even a small top-off of untreated tap water exposes gill tissue and filter bacteria to chlorine or chloramine.
  • Going by the calendar instead of by nitrate. A fixed weekly 10% change might be plenty for a lightly stocked planted tank and nowhere near enough for an overstocked one — test rather than assume.
  • One huge "catch-up" change on a neglected tank. This is the single riskiest mistake on this page — see old tank syndrome above.
  • Mismatched temperature water, repeated change after change. A one-time miss is usually fine; doing it every week adds up to chronic stress.
  • Vacuuming every inch of substrate every time. Rotating sections instead avoids disturbing the whole bacterial bed at once.
How much water should I change, and how often?

Most community tanks do well with 10-25% weekly. Go toward the higher end and more often for heavily stocked or large-fish tanks, and toward the lower end for lightly stocked, heavily planted tanks that use less of the water column's nutrients as fertilizer. There's no single universal number — nitrate testing tells you if your current schedule is actually keeping up.

Do I need to add dechlorinator every single time?

Yes, every time you add tap water, even a small top-off. Chlorine and chloramine are added to municipal water specifically to kill bacteria, and they don't know the difference between the bacteria in a treatment plant and the beneficial bacteria colonizing your filter. Well water that hasn't been chemically treated may not need it, but it's still worth testing once to confirm.

Can a water change be too big?

Yes, in two different ways. On an established, well-maintained tank, a large change can swing temperature and mineral content enough to stress fish, and it uses up dissolved oxygen as the dechlorinator neutralizes chlorine/chloramine. On a long-neglected tank, a single huge change can crash the stable (if poor) chemistry the fish have adjusted to, which is more dangerous than the neglect itself. Gradual is safer in both cases.

What is old tank syndrome and how do I fix it?

It's what happens when a tank goes too long between adequate water changes: nitrate climbs, pH and buffering (KH) drift down, and the fish slowly adjust to water quality that would stress or kill a fish just added to it. Fix it gradually — about 25% every day or every other day until parameters normalize — rather than one dramatic change, which can shock fish that have adapted to the degraded water.

Does a water change replace testing?

No. A water change dilutes whatever has built up, but it doesn't tell you what built up or how fast. Testing nitrate (and pH/KH if you suspect drift) is what tells you whether your current schedule is actually enough, especially after changing stocking, feeding, or plant load.

Why does my tank look cloudy right after a water change?

A brief haze from disturbed substrate or dissolved gas in fresh tap water coming out of solution as it warms is normal and clears within hours. If it doesn't clear, or keeps happening the same way every time, see our cloudy water guide for the other causes.
