Aquarium Maintenance Schedule: A Realistic Routine

By Pet Zone SD TeamUpdated October 7, 202610 min read

Most aquarium problems that look sudden — an algae bloom, a stressed fish, a cloudy tank — are actually the slow result of a maintenance routine that drifted, not a single mistake. "Clean it when it looks dirty" fails because the things that matter most (rising nitrate, a slowing filter, a drifting pH) don't look like anything until they're already a problem. A short, consistent routine catches them while they're still boring. This guide lays out what to actually do, and how often, without turning your tank into a part-time job.

Daily (two minutes, most days)

  • Feed an appropriate amount — what fish clear in under two minutes, not a fixed scoop regardless of appetite.
  • Glance at behavior and equipment. Is everyone swimming normally? Is the filter running, the heater's indicator light on, and the temperature where it should be?
  • Note anything odd — a fish hiding more than usual, cloudy water, a smell — rather than waiting for the weekly check to notice a pattern.

Weekly

  • Partial water change, roughly 10-25% depending on stocking density, with dechlorinated, temperature-matched water.
  • Test ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate on anything newer than a few months, or anything you're troubleshooting — see our full water parameters and testing guide for what the numbers mean and how often established tanks really need checking.
  • Wipe algae off the front glass and spot-check decor; a little algae elsewhere is normal and, in a shrimp tank, actually useful food — see our algae guide if it's spreading rather than just present.
  • Light substrate vacuum of visible debris during the water change, without digging deep into the bed.
  • Trim fast-growing plants that are shading others or need thinning.

Monthly

  • Rinse filter media only if flow has visibly slowed — and always in removed tank water, never tap water. Chlorine and chloramine in tap water kill the same bacteria colony your cycle depends on; see our cycling guide for why that colony matters and how slowly it recovers if you wipe it out.
  • Deeper gravel vacuum, working through sections of the substrate over a few sessions rather than disturbing the whole bed at once.
  • Check and clean intake and outflow on filters and powerheads, where flow restriction shows up first.
  • Inspect hardscape and decor for algae buildup, instability, or anything degrading (some woods and rocks shift as they settle — see our driftwood and rocks prep guide if you're adding or replacing a piece).
  • Dose root tabs or liquid fertilizer on a planted tank's normal schedule — see our beginner planted tank guide for a starting fertilizing routine.

Quarterly / seasonal

  • Replace aging light bulbs/LEDs if output has visibly dimmed or on the manufacturer's rated lifespan, since fading light quietly weakens plant growth and can shift algae balance.
  • Inspect tubing, impellers, and seals on filters and heaters for wear before they fail outright.
  • Confirm your heater and thermometer still agree with each other — heaters can drift off their dial setting over time.
  • Review stocking against current bioload: have fish grown, bred, or been added since your last real stocking check? See our fish compatibility guide before adding anything new.
  • Replace test kit reagents that are past due — expired reagents give unreliable readings without any obvious sign they've gone bad.

Aquarium Science's review of water-change practice makes the same point from a different angle: smaller, more frequent changes outperform large infrequent ones for keeping dissolved waste and parameter swings under control, even when the total monthly water volume changed ends up similar (Aquarium Science, Aquarium Water Changes).

Common maintenance mistakes

  • Overcleaning filter media — rinsing it on a fixed schedule "to keep it fresh" instead of only when flow actually slows, which can set a cycle back by weeks.
  • Treating a top-off as a water change. Topping off evaporated water replaces volume, not the nitrate and organics a real water change removes.
  • Big, infrequent water changes instead of smaller weekly ones — especially risky for shrimp, which are sensitive to sudden GH/KH shifts; see our Neocaridina shrimp care guide on why gradual changes matter so much for them.
  • Deep-cleaning everything at once — gravel, filter, and decor all disturbed the same day removes more beneficial bacteria and biofilm than a tank can comfortably lose in one sitting.
  • Skipping testing until something looks wrong, rather than using a short routine to catch drift before livestock show symptoms.

If your routine keeps slipping because it feels like guesswork, start by just testing weekly and doing a modest 10-15% water change on the same day each week. Most of the rest of this schedule exists to support that one habit, not replace it.

How often should I do a water change?

Roughly 10-25% weekly works for most community tanks, adjusted up for heavily stocked tanks and down for lightly stocked, heavily planted ones. Smaller, more frequent changes are gentler on fish and shrimp than large infrequent ones.

Do I need to clean my filter media every week?

No — and doing so can hurt more than help. Rinse media only when flow is visibly reduced, and always rinse it in removed tank water, never tap water, since chlorine and chloramine kill the bacteria colony living on it.

What's the difference between a top-off and a real water change?

A top-off replaces water lost to evaporation and doesn't remove any dissolved waste, since nothing evaporates except water itself. A real water change removes a portion of tank water (and the nitrate, organics, and debris in it) and replaces it with fresh, dechlorinated water.

How do I know if I'm over- or under-maintaining my tank?

Consistently zero ammonia and nitrite with controlled, not-skyrocketing nitrate between changes is the sign of a routine that's working. Frequent algae blooms or rising nitrate suggest under-maintenance; a tank that never seems biologically stable or constantly stressed fish after routine tasks can be a sign of overcleaning.

Should I vacuum the substrate every week?

A light vacuum of visible debris during the weekly water change is reasonable for most tanks. A deep, thorough gravel vacuum is better done monthly rather than weekly, since substrate also hosts a useful amount of beneficial bacteria you don't want to disturb constantly.

Can I skip maintenance for a week or two?

An established, lightly stocked, well-planted tank can usually tolerate a missed week without real harm. A newer, more heavily stocked, or shrimp tank has much less margin, and skipped testing or water changes show up faster as rising nitrate, algae, or stressed livestock.
