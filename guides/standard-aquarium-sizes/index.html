Standard Aquarium Sizes: Dimensions & Weight Chart

By Pet Zone SD TeamUpdated November 9, 20269 min read

A "20-gallon" aquarium isn't one tank — it's at least two, with meaningfully different footprints, and a filled 55-gallon weighs close to 650 pounds once water, glass, and substrate are all counted. Before you buy a tank, a stand, or a background, it's worth knowing the real dimensions and weight of the size you're considering, not just the gallon number on the box. This guide covers the common standard sizes, why shape matters as much as volume, and what the weight actually means for where a tank can safely go.

Standard aquarium sizes at a glance

These are the dimensions and weights most U.S. manufacturers build to for a standard rectangular glass aquarium. Treat them as close estimates rather than exact figures for every brand — see the note on brand variation further down.

Why two tanks with the same gallon count aren't the same tank

Gallon count tells you volume, not shape — and shape decides how much usable floor space and surface area you actually get. A 20 gallon long (30 x 12 x 12 in) has a 360 square-inch footprint; a 20 gallon high (24 x 12 x 16 in) has only 288 square inches, despite holding the identical 20 gallons. That extra floor space in the long tank means more room for bottom-dwelling fish to spread out and more water-surface area for oxygen exchange, which is why long and low tanks are generally the better pick for active swimmers and schooling fish, while high tanks suit tall plants, driftwood-heavy aquascapes, or a narrower footprint in a tight space.

The same logic shows up again at 40 gallons: a 40 gallon breeder (36 x 18 x 16 in) is shorter but much wider than a 40 gallon long (48 x 12 x 16 in), giving the breeder roughly 648 square inches of floor space against the long's 576. That's exactly why "breeder" tanks are popular for raising fry, keeping bottom-heavy species like larger cichlids, or any project where swimming area and sightlines matter more than a long, narrow display. See our fish breeding basics guide for how a breeder tank's shape gets used in practice.

Weight: the part most first-time buyers underestimate

Water alone weighs about 8.3 lbs per US gallon, but a tank's real filled weight runs higher once the glass, substrate, and any rockwork are added — commonly estimated at roughly 10-11 lbs per gallon total. That puts a filled 55-gallon tank in the 625-683 lb range and a filled 75-gallon well over 800 lbs, concentrated onto whatever is directly underneath it. A 125-gallon setup can clear 1,400 lbs — more than a loaded refrigerator and several adults combined, sitting in one spot.

That weight is also why placement matters, not just stand strength: a tank centered over a floor joist, or on a ground floor over a slab, is a very different load case than the same tank on an upper floor near the middle of a long, unsupported span. For anything in the 75-gallon-and-up range on a floor other than a slab-on-grade ground floor, it's worth a quick check with a contractor or structural engineer rather than assuming the floor can take it — this guide covers buying and sizing, not structural engineering.

Rule of thumb: get the stand rated specifically for your tank's filled weight, not its dry weight — a stand "good for 100 lbs" isn't good for a 10-gallon tank once it's actually running.

Picking a stand that's actually built for the job

Regular furniture — bookshelves, dressers, side tables — is built for weight spread across a shelf or distributed load, not the concentrated, 24/7 static load of a filled aquarium sitting on feet or a continuous rim. Manufacturers rate aquarium stands for a specific filled weight for a reason: a stand that flexes, racks, or has a slightly uneven top can crack a tank's seams or send it sliding off-level over months, not just in a single dramatic failure. Match the stand's rated capacity to your tank's filled weight from the table above, confirm the floor underneath is level before setup, and see our aquarium setup and equipment guide for the rest of what belongs on day one alongside tank and stand.

Gallons to liters, for anyone shopping internationally

Multiply US gallons by 3.785 to get liters: a 10-gallon tank is about 38 liters, a 29-gallon is about 110 liters, and a 55-gallon is about 208 liters. If you're comparing against a product listed in imperial gallons (common in UK and some Commonwealth retailers), use 4.546 liters per imperial gallon instead — an imperial gallon holds about 20% more than a US gallon, so a "40 gallon" tank specified in imperial units is a noticeably bigger tank than a US 40.

Common sizing mistakes

  • Buying by footprint alone without checking weight — a tank that fits the available floor space can still be too heavy for the furniture or floor underneath it.
  • Assuming "20 gallons" means one shape. Always check whether a listing is long, high, or another variant before buying a background, canopy, or light fixture sized to fit.
  • Sizing a stand to the tank's empty weight instead of its filled weight, which is 8-10x higher once water goes in.
  • Not checking doorway and stairwell clearance before buying a large tank — a 48-inch-long tank can be awkward or impossible to maneuver through a standard interior doorway; see our guide to moving an aquarium if you're relocating one rather than buying new.
  • Treating every "standard" size as identical across brands. Confirm the exact model's listed dimensions, especially glass thickness and trim, before ordering a custom-fit lid, background, or sump plumbing.
What's the difference between a 20 gallon long and a 20 gallon high?

Both hold 20 gallons, but a 20 long is about 30 x 12 x 12 inches and a 20 high is about 24 x 12 x 16 inches. The long has roughly 25% more floor footprint (360 vs. 288 square inches), which means more swimming room for bottom dwellers and schooling fish and more water-surface area for gas exchange, even though the two tanks hold the same volume.

How much does a filled 55-gallon aquarium actually weigh?

Around 625-683 pounds total once you include the empty tank, water, and substrate, depending on the exact model. Water alone runs about 8.3 lbs per gallon, but once gravel and the tank itself are added, the commonly used estimate for total filled weight is closer to 10-11 lbs per gallon.

Does a bigger tank always mean a bigger footprint?

No. A 40-gallon breeder (36 x 18 x 16 in) has more floor footprint than a 40-gallon long (48 x 12 x 16 in) despite holding the same volume, because it trades length for width. Shape, not just gallon count, decides how much floor space and swimming room a tank actually gives your fish.

Can I put a large aquarium on a regular piece of furniture instead of a rated stand?

Not safely past a small tank. A filled aquarium concentrates hundreds of pounds onto four small feet or a continuous edge, a load bookshelves and dressers aren't built to carry indefinitely. Use a stand the manufacturer rates for your tank's filled weight, and check with a contractor before placing anything over roughly 75 gallons on an upper floor.

Are "standard" aquarium dimensions the same across every brand?

Close, but not identical. Most manufacturers build to the same general gallon-and-shape conventions (a 29 gallon is always roughly 30 x 12-13 x 18-19 inches), but exact measurements, glass thickness, and weight can vary a bit by brand. Always check the specific model's listed dimensions before buying a stand, canopy, or background sized to fit.

How do I convert gallons to liters for an aquarium?

Multiply US gallons by 3.785 to get liters — a 10-gallon tank is about 38 liters, and a 55-gallon tank is about 208 liters. Imperial gallons (used in the UK) convert at 4.546 liters each instead, so a tank listed in imperial gallons will hold noticeably more than the same number in US gallons.
