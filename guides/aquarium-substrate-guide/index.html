Aquarium Substrate Guide: Gravel, Sand & Aquasoil Compared

By Pet Zone SD TeamUpdated November 6, 20269 min read

For a fish-only tank, plain gravel wins on ease of cleaning and cost. For a planted tank, aquasoil's ability to hold nutrients in the root zone outperforms gravel or sand on their own. For bottom-dwelling fish and most shrimp, fine sand is gentler underfoot and shows waste more clearly than gravel. None of the three is a universal best choice—each one trades off nutrients, cleaning method, and first-cycle behavior differently, and picking the wrong one for your plan causes more stalled plants and cloudy-water panic than almost any other early decision. This guide breaks down what each substrate actually does chemically and mechanically, not just what the bag claims. If you haven't settled on equipment yet, start with our setup & equipment guide; if you're planning a planted tank specifically, pair this with your first planted tank.

Aquasoil: what "nutrient-rich" actually means

Marketing copy calls aquasoil "nutrient-rich," but the more precise mechanism is cation exchange capacity (CEC)—essentially how sticky a substrate is for nutrients. Aquasoil's clay and organic particles carry a net negative charge, so they attract and hold onto positively charged nutrient ions like ammonium, iron, and trace elements instead of letting them wash away into the water column. Plant roots then release hydrogen ions into the substrate and trade them for those bound nutrients, pulling a steady supply directly from the grains around them (The 2Hr Aquarist, Planted Aquarium Substrate: Active vs. Inert). Plants also use ammonium more efficiently than nitrate, so a substrate that holds ammonium in the root zone rather than letting it diffuse away is a real functional advantage, not just a sales point.

The same humic acids and ion-exchange process that make this work also tend to lower pH to roughly 5.5-6.8 and soften KH, which suits most tropical plants and soft-water fish but is worth testing for rather than assuming (Aqua Essentials, Aqua Soil vs. Sand vs. Gravel). Budget on roughly 2-3 years before the easily available nutrient reserve runs low—root tabs or liquid fertilizer dosing after that point extend the substrate's useful life well past that (Buce Plant, Planted Aquarium Substrate: Soil, Gravel, and Sand). Keep at least 4cm (about 1.5 inches) of soil at the bottom for roots to actually establish in (Buce Plant).

The aquasoil ammonia spike (and why it's not a defect)

A new high-nutrient aquasoil releasing ammonia in week one isn't a bad batch—it's the same mechanism that will feed your plants for the next two years doing exactly what it's supposed to do, just before any fish are in the tank to be hurt by it.

High-ammonia aquasoils can push water ammonia above 10 ppm within a day or two of first filling the tank (The 2Hr Aquarist). The fix is straightforward: run 25-50% water changes roughly twice a week for the first 2-4 weeks, test ammonia and nitrite rather than guessing, and don't add fish until both read zero (Aqua Essentials)—in effect, treat it as the same fishless cycle covered in our tank cycling guide, just with the substrate itself as an early ammonia source instead of (or alongside) a bottled bacteria starter.

One thing worth knowing before you add soil to a brand-new tank: aquasoil cannot be gravel-vacuumed the way inert substrates can (Aqua Essentials). Debris gets siphoned off the surface and removed during water changes instead of dug out of the substrate itself, which is part of why a planted aquasoil tank's maintenance routine looks different from a bare gravel fish-only setup—see our maintenance schedule guide for how that changes the weekly routine.

Sand: inert, gentle on bottom-dwellers, and the compaction risk

Sand carries essentially no nutrients and doesn't affect water chemistry on its own. Its real advantage is physical: fine grain is ideal for bottom-feeders and fish with soft bellies, which is why it's a common recommendation for corydoras, loaches, and many dwarf shrimp (Buce Plant). The tradeoff is cleaning. A deep, undisturbed sand bed can compact and go anaerobic, producing hydrogen sulfide pockets that turn black and smell unmistakably bad when disturbed. Keep the bed shallow—around 2.5cm (1 inch)—and either hover a gravel vacuum slightly above the surface (fine sand lifts easily and will get sucked up with the debris if you dig in) or run your fingers through it regularly between water changes to keep it turned over (Swell UK, How to Clean Aquarium Sand). Always rinse new sand thoroughly in a bucket before adding it; unrinsed sand clouds a tank for days (Swell UK).

Sand can still support a planted tank with root tabs, but because it holds no nutrients of its own, tabs leach into the water column rather than staying bound in the root zone the way they would in aquasoil—regular water-column dosing ends up doing more of the work (The 2Hr Aquarist). See our plant fertilizer guide for dosing routines that work with an inert substrate.

Gravel: the easiest to clean, but a dead end for heavy feeders

Gravel is chemically inert like sand but heavier, which makes it the easiest of the three to gravel-vacuum—debris lifts into the siphon while the substrate itself settles right back down (Swell UK). Grain shape matters more than most beginners expect: sharp or jagged gravel can cut a bottom-feeder's face or barbels while it forages, and those small cuts invite bacterial infection, so rounded gravel is the safer pick for corydoras and loaches (Buce Plant).

On its own, gravel is a dead end for anything beyond the hardiest rooted plants—anubias, java fern, cryptocorynes, and amazon swords tolerate it, but heavy feeders and carpeting plants stall without supplementation (Aqua Essentials). Root tabs pushed in near individual plants are the simplest fix; a thin nutrient-rich base layer under the visible gravel cap (below) is the better fix for a tank with more than a few rooted plants.

Capping and mixing substrates

Using more than one substrate in the same tank is a standard aquascaping move, not a compromise. A common layout places a 1-2cm nutrient-rich base layer on the bottom, capped with 3-5cm of inert gravel or sand—roots reach down through the cap into the nutrient layer, while the visible cap stays the color and texture you actually want on top (Aqua Essentials). Soil toward the back with sand visible at the front is another common split, letting plants root in soil while the foreground stays clean-looking (Buce Plant).

Either way, separate the two substrates with hardscape—rock or prepared driftwood—rather than letting them touch directly, since loose substrates mix together over time without a physical barrier (Buce Plant). Don't scatter fine sand and coarse gravel together in the open, either—the heavier gravel settles downward and buries the sand over time rather than staying mixed where you put it.

Common substrate mistakes

  • Buying by color alone without checking whether it feeds plants, affects pH, or suits the fish and shrimp actually going in the tank.
  • Adding fish right after filling a tank with fresh aquasoil, before the initial ammonia spike has been tested down to zero.
  • Deep-vacuuming a sand bed the same way you would gravel, which just pulls the sand up with the debris instead of cleaning it.
  • Letting a sand bed sit undisturbed for months, risking anaerobic, hydrogen-sulfide pockets that only show up when something finally stirs them.
  • Planting heavy feeders in bare gravel with no root tabs or base layer, then blaming the plant when it stalls.
  • Mixing sand and gravel with no barrier between them, expecting the layered look to hold on its own.
What's the real difference between gravel, sand, and aquasoil?

Gravel and sand are chemically inert—they hold no nutrients and don't affect pH or hardness. Aquasoil is an "active" substrate: its clay and organic particles carry a negative charge that attracts and holds positively charged nutrients like ammonium, iron, and trace elements in the root zone, and it typically softens water and lowers pH as a side effect. Gravel and sand need root tabs or a nutrient base layer to support heavy-feeding plants; aquasoil feeds them on its own for a few years.

Why does aquasoil cause an ammonia spike, and what do I do about it?

High-nutrient aquasoils release some of their stored ammonium into the water as they settle, which can push ammonia above 10 ppm within the first couple of days in an uncycled tank. Run large, frequent water changes (roughly 25-50% twice a week) for the first 2-4 weeks, test ammonia and nitrite before adding anything, and treat it as a fishless cycle—don't add fish until both read zero.

Can I grow a planted tank in plain gravel or sand?

Yes, with help. Epiphytes like anubias, java fern, and bucephalandra are tied to wood or rock and need no substrate nutrients at all. True root feeders need root tabs pushed in near them, or a thin nutrient-rich base layer capped with 3-5cm of gravel or sand so roots can reach down to it. It works well for hardy rooted plants; it's a weaker setup than aquasoil for heavy-feeding carpeting plants.

How do I clean sand without clouding the whole tank?

Keep the bed shallow (around 2.5cm/1 inch) and hold a gravel vacuum slightly above the sand rather than burying the tube in it—the lighter grains lift, get cleaned, and settle back down instead of getting sucked up with the debris. Between vacuums, run your fingers through the sand to turn it over, which also helps avoid the anaerobic, hydrogen-sulfide-producing pockets that undisturbed deep sand beds are prone to.

Can I mix two substrate types in the same tank?

Yes—soil or a nutrient base layer toward the back or under a cap, with sand or gravel visible at the front or on top, is a common aquascaping layout. Separate the two with hardscape (rock or driftwood) rather than letting them touch directly, since otherwise they mix together over time; don't combine fine sand with coarser gravel in the open, either, since the heavier gravel settles on top and buries the sand.

How long does aquasoil actually last before it needs replacing?

Most aquasoils supply meaningful nutrition for roughly 2-3 years before the easily available reserve is used up, though the physical substrate itself can last indefinitely if it isn't breaking down into mush. Root tabs or liquid fertilizer dosing after that point extend its useful life well past the point most keepers would otherwise tear a tank down and re-soil it.
