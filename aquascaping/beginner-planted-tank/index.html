Your First Planted Tank

A beginner planted tank doesn't need CO2 injection, pressurized fertilizer dosing, or exotic plants to succeed. Choose a nutrient-rich substrate, 6-8 hours of moderate light, and a handful of low-light, low-maintenance species, and you'll have a genuinely thriving planted tank without a steep learning curve.

Written by the Pet Zone SD Team · Updated September 2026

The four decisions that matter most

Every planted tank comes down to four variables working together: substrate, light, carbon (CO2 or lack of it), and plant selection. Get these roughly matched to each other and most beginner problems — algae, melting plants, slow growth — simply don't happen.

Choosing a substrate

Substrate typeBest forNotes
Nutrient-rich aquasoilMost beginnersFeeds root systems for months without extra dosing; can affect pH/hardness initially
Plain gravel or sand + root tabsBudget setups, hardscape-heavy layoutsInert; you control nutrients directly with root tabs and liquid fertilizer
Gravel with a nutrient layer underneathLarger tanksCheaper than a full aquasoil substrate at scale

Lighting without overcomplicating it

Aim for a fixture rated for planted tanks (most LED fixtures marketed for freshwater are fine) run for 6-8 hours a day on a timer. More intense or longer lighting doesn't grow plants meaningfully faster once you're in a reasonable range — it mostly raises the risk of algae outcompeting your plants for available nutrients.

Do you need CO2?

Not for a first tank. A well-chosen list of low-light, low-tech-friendly plants will grow well on the CO2 naturally dissolved in aquarium water:

  • Anubias (attach to hardscape, don't bury the rhizome)
  • Java fern (same rule — rhizome stays above substrate)
  • Cryptocoryne species
  • Java moss and other mosses
  • Amazon sword (heavier root feeder — benefits from aquasoil or root tabs)

CO2 injection is worth adding later if you want faster-growing stem plants or a denser, more demanding aquascape — but it adds a real maintenance and monitoring burden that isn't necessary to enjoy a first planted tank.

A simple beginner plant list

Six to eight plants is plenty for a first attempt: 2-3 anubias or java fern specimens attached to driftwood or rock, a cryptocoryne group in the midground, java moss on hardscape for texture, and one or two amazon swords or similar larger background plants.

Buying plants in person lets you check for snail eggs, melting leaves, and root health before they go in your tank — worth doing at a local fish store if you have one nearby, since online plant shipments are more prone to transit stress.

First-month expectations

Expect some transplant "melt" on certain species (especially anubias and crypts) in the first 1-2 weeks as they shed leaves grown in a different environment before pushing new growth. This is normal and not a sign of failure — resist the urge to remove the plant.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need CO2 injection for a beginner planted tank?

No. Many low-to-medium light plants (anubias, java fern, crypts, java moss) do well on ambient CO2 alone. CO2 injection becomes worthwhile once you move into higher light and demanding stem plants.

What substrate is best for beginner planted tanks?

A nutrient-rich aquasoil (such as ADA Aquasoil, UNS Controsoil, or similar) gives root-feeding plants a strong start without needing separate root tabs immediately. Plain gravel works too if you add root tabs for heavier root feeders.

How many hours of light does a planted tank need?

6-8 hours per day is a typical starting point. More hours doesn't mean more growth beyond that point — it mainly increases algae risk, so consistency matters more than duration.

Why is my new planted tank getting algae?

New tank algae is usually a light/nutrient imbalance while plants are still establishing, not a sign you did something wrong. Reducing light duration and keeping to a consistent fertilizing and water-change schedule typically resolves it within a few weeks.

Can I add fish and plants to a new tank at the same time?

The tank still needs to be cycled before fish go in, regardless of whether it's planted. Plants can go in before or during cycling since they help absorb some ammonia, but fish should wait until the cycle is complete.
